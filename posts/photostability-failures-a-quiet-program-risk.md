Photostability testing is a standard requirement in drug development. ICH Q1B outlines how to evaluate the effect of visible and UV light on drug substances and drug products, using both forced-degradation and confirmatory studies.

In many programs, photostability is treated as a compliance activity: run the required exposures, confirm that the product is “stable enough,” and move on.

The problem is that light-induced degradation can be subtle, cumulative, and easy to underestimate until it has already affected clinical material.

### How photostability failures show up

Light exposure can drive several degradation pathways, including photolysis, photooxidation, and isomerisation. These changes can:

* Reduce potency or alter activity.  
* Increase impurities or create new degradation products.  
* Affect appearance, colour, or clarity.  
* In some cases, generate products with different toxicological or allergenic potential.

For biologics, light sensitivity can affect structure and function, with consequences for quality and safety. For small molecules, photodegradation can be a dominant instability pathway, particularly for molecules with conjugated systems or known photosensitivity.

Failures rarely announce themselves dramatically. More often they appear as:

* Unexpected out-of-spec results during stability or release testing.  
* Higher-than-anticipated impurity levels in clinical batches.  
* Changes in appearance that trigger investigations or patient concerns.  
* The need for additional studies, re-packaging, or re-labelling late in development.

### Where the risk is created

Photostability risk is often built into the program before the first confirmatory study is run.

Common contributors include:

* **Limited forced-degradation understanding.** Without a clear picture of which parts of the molecule or formulation are photolabile, it is difficult to design appropriate controls or packaging.
* **Underestimating real-world exposure.** Manufacturing areas, inspection steps, transport, warehouse lighting, and clinical-site handling can all contribute to cumulative light exposure.
* **Packaging and labelling assumptions.** Decisions to use clear glass, standard closures, or minimal protective labelling may be made without data on how much protection is actually required.
* **Different light sources.** Switching between fluorescent, LED, or other lighting can change the spectral distribution and therefore the degradation profile, particularly for light-sensitive compounds.

The result can be a clinical batch that technically “passed” photostability testing under one set of conditions but is more vulnerable in practice.

### A more useful photostability strategy

A robust photostability approach should connect three elements:

**1. Mechanism and sensitivity**\
Forced-degradation studies should identify whether the drug substance or product is photolabile, which wavelengths are most relevant, and what degradation products are formed. This informs whether the molecule is inherently sensitive and what to watch for analytically.

**2. Realistic exposure scenarios**\
Confirmatory studies should reflect realistic conditions across the lifecycle: manufacturing, filling, inspection, packaging, transport, storage, and clinical use. This includes considering different light sources and handling practices that the product may encounter.

**3. Controls that match the risk**\
Depending on the findings, appropriate controls may include:

* Protective packaging (e.g., amber containers, opaque blisters).  
* Specific labelling on light protection and handling.  
* Defined limits on light exposure during manufacturing and distribution.  
* Analytical methods capable of detecting relevant photodegradation products.

The objective is not to over-engineer protection for every molecule. It is to ensure that the level of control is proportionate to the actual photostability risk.

### From quiet risk to managed risk

Photostability should be considered alongside other stability risks early in development, not as a late-stage testing requirement.

Key questions include:

* Is the molecule or formulation known or suspected to be light-sensitive?  
* What do forced-degradation studies reveal about photolability and degradation pathways?  
* Are analytical methods suitable to detect relevant photodegradation products?  
* Do packaging and labelling decisions reflect the data, or are they based on convention?  
* How much light exposure is realistic across manufacturing, transport, and clinical use?  
* What would a photostability failure look like for this product, and how would it affect clinical supply?
