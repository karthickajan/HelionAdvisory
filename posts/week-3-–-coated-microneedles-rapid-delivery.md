## **What are coated microneedles?**

Coated microneedles are an evolution of the solid microneedle platform.

A coated microneedle array comprises sharp micrometer‑sized needle shafts attached to a base substrate, with the drug coated on their surfaces. The coating can be applied using a variety of methods, including dip coating, spray coating, electrohydrodynamic atomisation (EHDA), and piezoelectric inkjet printing.

Upon insertion into the skin, the coating dissolves rapidly, releasing the drug into the microchannels created by the needles—bypassing the stratum corneum and enabling efficient absorption.

This “coat and poke” approach eliminates the two‑step application of solid microneedles and enables precise, rapid delivery of low‑dose biotherapeutics, vaccines, peptides, and hormones.

## **How coated microneedles are used**

Coated microneedles are particularly well suited for:

* **Vaccines:** Rapid delivery of antigens and adjuvants, often with improved stability compared with liquid formulations.
* **Peptides and hormones:** Precise delivery of low‑dose biotherapeutics that are difficult to administer orally. 
* **Small molecules:** Rapid delivery of potent drugs where a small, controlled dose is sufficient.
* **Cosmetics and dermatology:** Targeted delivery of cosmetic actives or dermatological agents.

## **Advantages of coated microneedles**

Coated microneedles offer several advantages over other microneedle types and conventional delivery methods:

* **One‑step application.** No separate patch or gel needed—simplifies use and reduces dosing variability.
* **Rapid release.** Coating dissolves within minutes, delivering drug quickly into the skin.
* **Precise dose control.** Coating formulation and thickness can be tuned to control dose per needle.
* **Preserves bioactivity.** Coating avoids exposing drug to harsh processing conditions, helping preserve bioactivity of sensitive molecules.
* **Versatile.** Suitable for vaccines, peptides, hormones, and other low‑dose biotherapeutics.

## **Challenges and limitations**

Despite their advantages, coated microneedles come with several challenges:

* **Limited drug‑loading capacity.** The small surface area of the needle shafts restricts how much drug can be coated, making coated MNs best suited for low‑dose therapies.
* **Coating uniformity and dose accuracy.** Achieving consistent coating thickness and drug distribution across an array is technically challenging; variability can lead to inaccurate dosing.
* **Coating loss or incomplete delivery.** Some coating may be lost during insertion or remain on the skin surface, reducing delivered dose.
* **Mechanical fragility.** The coating process can weaken the needle, increasing fracture risk during insertion, especially in dry skin.
* **Stability during storage and transport.** Coated formulations can be sensitive to moisture, temperature, and light, requiring careful packaging and storage conditions.

## **CMC and regulatory considerations**

For CMC teams, coated microneedles raise several important questions:

* **Coating method selection.** Techniques include dip coating, spray coating, EHDA, and piezoelectric inkjet printing—each with different implications for uniformity, scalability, and cost.
* **Dose control and content uniformity.** Precise dose per needle is a core CQA.
* **Coating characterisation.** Thickness, uniformity, adhesion, and drug distribution must be measured and controlled across the array.
* **Stability and packaging.** Coated MNs may require moisture‑barrier packaging and defined storage conditions to preserve coating integrity and drug stability.
* **Regulatory pathway.** Coated microneedles are typically regulated as combination products (device + drug), with expectations around dose uniformity, mechanical integrity, and clinical evidence still evolving.

## **Why coated microneedles matter for CMC**

Coated microneedles offer a compelling balance of simplicity and precision for low‑dose, rapid‑release applications—but their success depends heavily on getting the coating process and dose control right.

For sponsors, they raise distinct questions around:

* Formulation compatibility with needle materials and coating methods.
* Stability of drug in the coating during storage and transport.
* Device design, mechanical testing, and human‑factors engineering.
* Analytical methods to assess coating uniformity, dose per needle, and release kinetics.
* Scale‑up, packaging, and storage conditions that preserve both drug and device performance.
* Regulatory strategy for a combination product in a still‑maturing field.
