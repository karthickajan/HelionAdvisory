Process characterization is usually defined as a set of structured studies where key process parameters are deliberately varied to see how they affect product quality and process performance. In the process validation lifecycle, this work sits in Stage 1 – process design – and should directly inform the control strategy that carries into qualification and continued verification. But in practice, characterization can drift into an exercise in generating data rather than building understanding.

Regulators emphasise that process validation is about understanding sources of variation, their impact on product attributes, and how to detect and control that variation over the lifecycle. A well-run characterization programme therefore does more than run designs-of-experiments; it clarifies the role of each unit operation, the relationships between inputs and outputs, and which variables truly matter for quality and performance. When studies are treated as statistical checkboxes, teams can end up with large data sets and only a limited sense of what the process is actually telling them.

The difference becomes visible in outcomes:

* Data-only characterisation produces long reports, complex models, and parameter lists, but leaves teams uncertain which findings are robust, actionable, or critical.
* Understanding-focused characterisation yields clear narratives: which steps clear which impurities, which parameters drive yield and purity, and which ranges provide real operating flexibility without compromising CQAs.
* Programmes built on understanding navigate PPQ and commercial scale with fewer surprises, because control strategies reflect how the process behaves, not just where the data happened to land.

Designing characterization for understanding means:

* Starting with risk assessment and data mining to select parameters and ranges that matter most to process success and PPQ risk.
* Ensuring scale-down models and feed materials are representative of intended commercial conditions, so results translate into reality.
* Writing unit operation reports and summary documents that explain the “why” behind chosen CPPs, ranges, and controls – not just presenting statistical outputs.
