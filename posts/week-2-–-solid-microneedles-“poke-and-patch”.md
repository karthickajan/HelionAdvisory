## **What are solid microneedles?**

Solid microneedles are the first and simplest generation of microneedle technology.

They are typically micron‑scale needle structures, 50–900 µm in length, made from materials such as silicon, stainless steel, or biodegradable polymers. Their tip diameters range from 1 to 80 µm, allowing them to pierce the stratum corneum and reach the viable epidermis without stimulating deeper nerves or blood vessels.

Solid microneedles work on a two‑step “poke and patch” principle:

1. **Poke:** The solid array is pressed into the skin to create transient microchannels (micro‑pores) through the stratum corneum.
2. **Patch:** A drug formulation—gel, cream, patch, nanoparticle, or liquid—is applied over the treated area. The drug then diffuses through the newly created pores into the epidermis and dermis, and from there into systemic circulation.

Because the drug is applied separately from the microneedle array, solid microneedles offer flexibility in drug choice and formulation.

## **How solid microneedles are used**

Solid microneedles are primarily used for **skin pretreatment** to enhance the permeability of the skin for a wide range of drugs and biologics.

Applications include:

* **Vaccines and immunotherapies:** Enhancing delivery of antigens and adjuvants through the skin.
* **Peptides and proteins:** Enabling transdermal delivery of molecules that are poorly absorbed through intact skin.
* **Chemotherapy and dermatology:** Improving local delivery of anti‑cancer and dermatological agents.
* **Cosmetics and aesthetics:** Enhancing penetration of cosmetic actives (e.g., in microneedling rollers and dermarollers).
* **Diagnostics and monitoring:** Collecting interstitial fluid or blood for glucose, triglyceride, and mineral monitoring.

## **Advantages of solid microneedles**

Solid microneedles offer several advantages over other microneedle types and conventional delivery methods:

* **Simple and cost‑effective fabrication.** Solid arrays are easier and cheaper to manufacture than hollow or dissolving types, which is why most early microneedle studies used solid versions.
* **Flexibility in drug choice.** Because the drug is applied separately, a wide range of formulations (gels, creams, patches, liquids) can be used with the same microneedle array.
* **Good for skin pretreatment.** They enhance permeability for a wide variety of topical drugs, including those that are poorly absorbed through intact skin.
* **Painless application.** Because they only penetrate the outer skin layers, they avoid deeper nerves and blood vessels, making the process largely painless.

## **Challenges and limitations**

Despite their simplicity, solid microneedles come with several challenges:

* **Two‑step application.** The need to poke first and then apply drug adds a step that can introduce variability in dosing and adherence.
* **Pore closure kinetics.** The microchannels close relatively quickly, so the drug must be applied within a defined window for effective delivery; formulation properties and drug physicochemistry matter a lot.
* **Insertion variability.** Uneven skin surfaces or insufficient pressure can lead to incomplete insertion and low penetration efficiency, especially with flat arrays.
* **Risk of irritation or infection.** Repeated use or poor hygiene can increase the chance of irritation or infection at the application site.
* **Limited dose control.** Because the drug is applied separately, precise dosing depends on the subsequent formulation and application technique, not on the microneedle array itself.

## **CMC and regulatory considerations**

For CMC teams, solid microneedles raise several important questions:

* **Material selection and biocompatibility.** Choosing between metal, silicon, or biodegradable polymers affects mechanical strength, cost, and biocompatibility. Each material has different manufacturing and regulatory implications.
* **Array design and mechanical testing.** Needle geometry (length, tip diameter, aspect ratio) must be optimised to ensure consistent penetration without bending or breakage. Mechanical testing is essential to validate performance.
* **Sterilisation and cleaning protocols.** If the device is reusable, robust sterilisation and cleaning protocols are needed to prevent infection and cross‑contamination.
* **Regulatory classification.** Solid microneedles used for skin pretreatment may be regulated as devices under the FD&C Act, while the drug formulation is regulated separately, creating a combination‑product pathway. FDA guidance on microneedling products clarifies when a product is a device and what regulatory pathway applies.
* **Characterisation and quality control.** CQAs for solid microneedles include tip geometry, mechanical strength, insertion depth, and absence of defects. Analytical methods must be capable of assessing these attributes consistently.

## **Why solid microneedles matter for CMC**

Solid microneedles are the “workhorse” of the microneedle world: simple, flexible, and well understood—but not without their own CMC and regulatory nuances.

For sponsors, they raise distinct questions around:

* Formulation compatibility with needle materials and skin pretreatment protocols.
* Device design, mechanical testing, and human‑factors engineering.
* Analytical methods to assess array integrity, insertion depth, and skin permeability.
* Scale‑up, packaging, and storage conditions that preserve device performance.
* Regulatory strategy for a device‑only or combination‑product pathway.
