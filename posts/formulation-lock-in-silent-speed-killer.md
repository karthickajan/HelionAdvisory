## The promise of “lock it and go”

In early development, there is strong pressure to move quickly.

Teams often want to lock the formulation early so they can manufacture tox batches, then clinical material, without CMC becoming a bottleneck. The logic is clear: decide once, then focus on clinical execution.

But a formulation decision made too early—or on too little information—can become a silent speed killer later in the program.

## Why late formulation changes are expensive

Once clinical material has been manufactured using a specific formulation, any significant change creates a before-and-after scenario. Regulators will expect evidence that the change does not adversely affect product quality, safety, or efficacy

A formulation change can trigger:

* **Comparability studies.** Analytical comparisons, and in some cases functional or nonclinical bridging, to demonstrate that pre- and post-change material are highly similar and that any differences have no adverse impact.
* **New stability data.** Additional long-term, accelerated, and possibly in-use stability to support shelf life, storage, and handling for the new formulation.
* **Analytical rework.** Requalification or revalidation of methods, and potentially new stability-indicating assays, to ensure they are suitable for the changed product.
* **Regulatory impact.** Updated filings, responses to agency questions, and potential delays to pivotal trials or submission timelines. 
* **Supply and logistics.** New batches, possible waste of existing inventory, and revised packaging or labelling.

Individually, each of these may seem manageable. Together, they can shift timelines by months or even years.

## How formulation lock-in creates risk

The risk is not simply that the formulation will need to change. It is that the decision was made without enough information to be confident it would not need to change.

Common contributors include:

* **Limited stability understanding.** Locking excipients, pH, concentration, or dosage form before degradation pathways and sensitivities are well understood.
* **Insufficient attention to delivery and use.** Choosing a formulation that works for early batches but is not optimized for the intended device, route, dose, or patient population.
* **Scalability and manufacturability gaps.** A formulation that is feasible at small scale but difficult to reproduce, control, or scale for commercial manufacture.
* **Regulatory and comparability underestimation.** Assuming that a “minor” formulation adjustment will be easy to justify later, without planning for the data that will be required.

The outcome is often not a dramatic failure but a gradual slowing of the program as additional work accumulates.

## A more deliberate approach to formulation decisions

Speed is still important. The goal is to avoid speed that creates rework.

A more resilient approach includes:

* **Early, focused formulation screening.** Not an endless search for perfection, but enough exploration to identify robust, stable, and manufacturable options.
* **Linking formulation to the target product profile.** Considering dose, route, device, patient use, and storage conditions from the beginning, not as an afterthought.
* **Generating stability and compatibility data early.** Using forced degradation, stress studies, and early stability to understand risks before the formulation is fixed.
* **Planning for change.** Recognising that some evolution may be needed and ensuring that the initial formulation and data package do not make future changes unnecessarily difficult.

The question is not “Can we lock the formulation today?” It is “Do we know enough to lock it with confidence?”
