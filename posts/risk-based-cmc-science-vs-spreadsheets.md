## When risk management becomes a scoring exercise

Risk assessment is an essential part of CMC development. It helps teams identify potential hazards, evaluate their significance, and determine appropriate controls across the product lifecycle.

But risk-based CMC can lose its value when the process becomes dominated by spreadsheets.

Teams may spend considerable time listing failure modes, assigning numerical scores, and producing colour-coded risk matrices. The result can look rigorous while still leaving fundamental questions unanswered:

* What is the scientific basis for the risk?
* How much is known about the failure mode?
* Which product quality attributes could be affected?
* What evidence would change the assessment?
* Does the proposed control actually reduce the risk?

A numerical score is an aid to discussion. It is not a substitute for understanding.

## What ICH Q9(R1) adds

ICH Q9 provides principles and tools for applying quality risk management across pharmaceutical development and manufacturing. The revised guidance places particular emphasis on risk-based decision-making and on choosing an appropriate level of effort, formality, and documentation for each situation.

This matters because not every risk requires the same treatment.

The formality of a risk assessment should reflect factors such as:

* The importance of the decision.
* The degree of uncertainty.
* The complexity of the process or situation.
* The potential impact on product quality and patient risk.

A high-impact, poorly understood process parameter may warrant targeted experimentation, additional characterisation, or stronger controls. A low-impact parameter supported by extensive process knowledge may require less formal treatment.

The objective is proportionality—not uniform paperwork.

## From risk score to scientific decision

A useful CMC risk assessment should lead to a decision. That decision might be to:

* Generate additional data.
* Investigate a degradation or failure mechanism.
* Narrow or expand an operating range.
* Add or strengthen an in-process control.
* Improve an analytical method.
* Introduce supplier or material controls.
* Accept the residual risk with a documented rationale.
* Revisit the assessment after further development or manufacturing experience.

The important link is:

**risk → rationale → evidence → action → review**

For example, consider a process parameter that receives a high risk score because it may affect a critical quality attribute. The score alone does not tell the team what to do. The scientific assessment should ask whether there is a plausible mechanism, whether prior data are representative, whether the attribute can be detected reliably, and what study or control would reduce the uncertainty.

That is where CMC expertise matters.

## Data quality matters more than data volume

Risk decisions are only as strong as the evidence behind them. Relevant evidence may include:

* Development and engineering batch history.
* Process characterisation studies.
* Product quality and impurity data.
* Stability and stress studies.
* Raw-material and supplier knowledge.
* Scale-down model representativeness.
* Analytical method capability.
* Deviation and complaint trends.
* Prior platform or process knowledge.

More data are not automatically better. The right evidence is data that help explain how the product or process behaves and whether a proposed control is effective.

A large risk register built on weak assumptions may be less useful than a smaller, well-supported assessment that clearly identifies uncertainty and action.

## The role of spreadsheets

Spreadsheets are not the problem. They can be useful for:

* Capturing risk statements.
* Assigning owners and due dates.
* Tracking mitigation actions.
* Recording residual risk.
* Supporting review and communication.

The problem occurs when the spreadsheet becomes the decision rather than a record of the decision.

A mature risk-management process uses the tool to support scientific discussion. It does not allow the score to replace the discussion.

## Building better risk-based CMC

A science-led approach to CMC risk management should:

* Define the decision before selecting the risk tool.
* Identify the relevant product, process, and patient risks.
* Separate known risk from uncertainty.
* Use appropriate data to test assumptions.
* Match the formality of the assessment to importance, uncertainty, and complexity.
* Document why controls are sufficient—or why further work is needed.
* Review risks as the product, process, and knowledge base evolve.
