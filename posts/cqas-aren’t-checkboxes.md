## What a CQA is supposed to do

Critical Quality Attributes are a core element of Quality by Design. ICH Q8(R2) defines a CQA as:

> “A physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality.

In a well-run program, CQAs:

* Are derived from the Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP).
* Are linked to safety, efficacy, and intended use.
* Guide formulation, process, and analytical development.
* Inform the control strategy and lifecycle management.

In other words, CQAs are meant to focus attention on what truly matters for product quality and patient outcomes.

## When CQAs become checkboxes

In practice, CQA identification can drift into a documentation exercise.

Common patterns include:

* **Everything is critical.** Every attribute that can be measured—identity, assay, impurities, dissolution, particle size, moisture, sterility, endotoxin, appearance, and more—is labelled a CQA, regardless of actual impact on safety or efficacy.
* **The list grows, the rationale does not.** New attributes are added as they are discovered or requested, but the underlying scientific justification remains vague or generic.
* **Uniform effort, variable risk.** All CQAs receive similar levels of characterization and control, even though their potential impact on product quality differs substantially.
* **Weak linkage to mechanism.** The connection between an attribute, its root causes, and its clinical relevance is not clearly described or used to prioritise development work.

The result is a CQA list that looks comprehensive but does little to guide decisions.

## A science- and risk-based view of CQAs

ICH Q8 and Q9 emphasise that CQA identification should be based on sound science and quality risk management.

A more useful approach asks:

* What is the intended use and target product profile?
* Which attributes, if they vary, could affect safety, efficacy, or performance?
* What is the severity of harm if the attribute falls outside the desired range?
* What prior knowledge, data, or mechanistic understanding supports the assessment?
* How does the attribute relate to material attributes and process parameters?
* Which attributes truly require tight control, and which can be managed with routine monitoring?pqri+3

This does not mean ignoring attributes that are not labelled “critical.” It means distinguishing between:

* Attributes that are critical to safety and efficacy and therefore require strong understanding and control.
* Attributes that are important for quality but can be managed through standard specifications and monitoring.
* Attributes that are primarily descriptive or informational.

## From CQA list to development focus

A well-defined CQA set should influence real decisions:

* **Formulation:** Which excipients, pH, tonicity, or stabilisers are needed to protect the critical attributes?
* **Process:** Which unit operations and parameters most affect the CQAs, and therefore deserve deeper characterisation?
* **Analytics:** Which methods must be especially robust, specific, or stability-indicating for the CQAs?
* **Control strategy:** Where should in-process controls, release tests, and stability monitoring be concentrated?
* **Lifecycle:** Which CQAs are most relevant when making post-approval changes or assessing comparability?

If the CQA list does not change how you allocate effort, it is probably too long or too generic.

## Making CQAs useful again

A practical CQA exercise should:

* Start from the QTPP and intended use, not from the analytical menu.
* Use risk assessment to separate high-severity attributes from lower-risk ones.
* Document the scientific rationale for each CQA, including the link to safety, efficacy, or performance.
* Prioritise characterization and control effort based on actual risk, not label status.
* Revisit CQAs as knowledge grows, rather than treating the initial list as permanent.
