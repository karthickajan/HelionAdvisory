## **The operational risk that ends programs**

Biotech companies often fail for reasons that are visible early: weak clinical differentiation, safety concerns, or an unclear commercial path.

But another failure mode is equally real and more easily deferred: CMC.

CMC—Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls—determines whether a molecule can be reliably made at scale, meet quality expectations, and satisfy regulatory requirements. It is a leading cause of Complete Response Letters and approval delays.

Yet CMC is frequently the part of the business that boards and executive teams understand the least and govern the most loosely.

## **Common executive blind spots**

Several patterns recur across biotech leadership teams and investor discussions.

**1. “Our CDMO will handle it.”**
Outsourcing development and manufacturing is standard. But outsourcing does not remove sponsor responsibility. Relying entirely on a CDMO without strong in-house CMC oversight can lead to:

* Work that is not required or not aligned with regulatory expectations.
* Cost overruns that are not recognised until late.
* Limited ability to challenge assumptions or evaluate alternatives.
* Weak comparability and change-control positions when the program evolves.

**2. Under-hiring or mis-timing technical expertise**
CMC is sometimes treated as a generic technical function. In reality, it requires specific experience in process development, analytical strategy, quality systems, regulatory submissions, and lifecycle management.

Under-hiring CMC expertise, or hiring too late, often becomes apparent when:

* The FDA raises concerns that could have been anticipated.
* Trials are delayed because material cannot be supplied or released.
* Batches are lost or fail to meet specifications.
* Costs escalate as remediation work is required under time pressure.

**3. CMC as a technical detail, not a business priority**
CMC can consume 25–40% or more of a financing, yet it may receive less than 1% of executive or board meeting time.

When CMC is discussed only as a technical update, its impact on:

* Timelines and milestones.
* Cash burn and contingency needs.
* Regulatory strategy and negotiation leverage.
* Partnering, licensing, and exit value.

may be significantly underestimated.

**4. Infrequent or superficial CMC oversight**
Boards and investors often focus on clinical data, commercial plans, and financials. CMC may be mentioned briefly, if at all.

This can create a governance gap where:

* CMC risks are not explicitly reviewed at key decision points.
* Assumptions about scale-up, supply, and comparability are not challenged.
* Contingency plans for manufacturing or supply disruptions are not in place.
* The link between CMC decisions and company valuation is not recognised.

## **Why CMC deserves executive attention**

CMC is not only a technical requirement. It is a business enabler.

A strong CMC strategy can:

* Reduce the risk of late-stage surprises and regulatory delays.
* Improve the credibility of the program with regulators, partners, and investors.
* Support more realistic timelines and budgets.
* Create optionality for licensing, partnering, or acquisition.
* Increase confidence that clinical success can translate into a commercial product.

Conversely, weak CMC can undermine even the most promising science.

## **Questions executives should ask**

For boards, CEOs, and investors, useful CMC questions include:

* Who owns CMC strategy internally, and what is their level of experience?
* How is CMC represented in leadership and board discussions?
* What are the top three CMC risks to our next milestone, and how are they being mitigated?
* How do our CMC plans affect timelines, budget, and contingency needs?
* What is our plan for scale-up, tech transfer, and potential site or supplier changes?
* How would we respond to a major CMC-related regulatory observation or delay?
* Are we investing enough in CMC expertise relative to the risk and spend involved?

These questions do not require every executive to become a CMC expert. They do require treating CMC as a strategic topic.
